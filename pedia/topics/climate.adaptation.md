# Adaptation pathways

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Coastal adaptation is a continuing risk-management process rather than a one-time project; effective strategies combine protection, accommodation, ecosystem measures and retreat with monitoring, maintenance, finance, governance and triggers for adjustment as hazards and information change.

**Governing physics.** Relative sea-level rise raises baseline water levels and shifts wave, surge, erosion, groundwater and drainage hazards; compound events and geomorphic or ecosystem response determine local exposure, while intervention performance depends on deterioration, recovery and sediment or ecological feedbacks.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Useful controls include sea-level rise relative to freeboard, benefit-cost ratio, residual-risk fraction, adaptation lifetime relative to hazard-change timescale, lead-time-to-threshold ratio, avoided-loss fraction, robustness across scenarios and ecological recovery relative to disturbance interval.

**Major equations.** Decision analysis combines probabilistic hazard and damage functions, discounted life-cycle cost and benefit, dynamic programming or real-options logic, and robustness across scenarios. Adaptive pathways encode actions, thresholds, lead times and transfer points when objectives are no longer met.

**Typical methods.** Workflows co-produce objectives, map compound hazards and vulnerability, evaluate portfolios under multiple projections, include capital and maintenance costs and distributional impacts, identify thresholds and lead times, stress-test pathways, monitor indicators and pre-authorize adjustment or retreat options.

**Numerical models.** The set includes scenario-based hazard and pathway frameworks, building-level Monte Carlo damage simulation and dynamic programming. Nature-based evidence emphasizes forward-looking performance, maintenance and adaptive intervention rather than a single fixed design model.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence includes a global coastal-adaptation literature synthesis, Bay County building-level Monte Carlo damage and dynamic-programming analysis under four sea-level projections, and a review of living-shoreline utility under changing climatic conditions.

**Validated ranges.** Bay County findings apply to its building inventory and four projections; global and living-shoreline reviews support principles rather than universal local benefit-cost rankings. Transfer requires local hazards, institutions, ecosystems, costs and stakeholder objectives.

**Recent advances.** Recent advances integrate high-resolution exposure, probabilistic projections, adaptation tipping points, building-level digital twins, participatory pathway tools, natural-capital accounting, climate services and evaluation of implementation, maintenance and distributional outcomes.

**Disagreements.** Protection can reduce near-term risk but induce lock-in and residual catastrophe; retreat avoids exposure but faces social and political barriers. Nature-based measures add co-benefits and adaptability yet have uncertain extreme-event and long-term performance; hybrid portfolios often manage these tradeoffs.

**Limitations.** Deep climate uncertainty, incomplete compound hazards, uncertain damage and behavior, discounting, omitted distributional effects, institutional fragmentation, funding and maintenance gaps, path dependence and ecological nonstationarity can dominate apparently precise rankings.

**Open questions.** Priorities include equitable pathway design, endogenous development and migration, compound hazard and cascading infrastructure risk, finance and governance experiments, monitoring thresholds, adaptation effectiveness audits and comparable long-term evidence for hybrid and nature-based portfolios.

**Seminal papers.** Protect–accommodate–retreat frameworks organized response options; robust decision making, real options and dynamic adaptive policy pathways shifted planning from fixed forecasts toward sequences that remain adjustable under uncertainty.

## Claims

- **C184.** A 2025 review concludes that durable living-shoreline performance under climate change requires forward-looking design, coordinated holistic planning, and maintenance and adaptive interventions, because present-day success does not establish resistance or recovery under future extremes. *Regime: Conceptual guidance across nature-based and hybrid shoreline types; no universal quantitative threshold is claimed..* [literature_review_statement, review] (DiPetto 2025, [doi:10.1016/j.nbsj.2025.100285](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nbsj.2025.100285))
- **C189.** A global coastal-adaptation review concludes that long-term plans must combine locally relevant sea-level projections with compound hazards, agreed action thresholds, stakeholder priorities, finance, and maintenance capacity; construction alone does not establish durable adaptation. *Regime: Strategic planning across diverse coasts; local design values require site-specific analysis..* [literature_review_statement, review] (Griggs 2021, [doi:10.3390/w13162151](https://doi.org/10.3390/w13162151))
- **C1135.** Australian vegetated coastal ecosystems store an estimated 70–185 Tg C above ground and 1,055–1,540 Tg C in the upper metre of soil; ongoing losses could emit 2.1–3.1 Tg CO2-e per year. *Regime: Australian vegetated coastal ecosystems as global hotspots for climate change mitigation.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Óscar Serrano 2019, [doi:10.1038/s41467-019-12176-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12176-8))
- **C1136.** Projected global coastal-wetland loss by 2100 ranges from 0 to 30% without added accommodation space; resilience is governed primarily by space for landward migration, which infrastructure can constrain and adaptation can restore. *Regime: Future response of global coastal wetlands to sea-level rise.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Mark Schuerch 2018, [doi:10.1038/s41586-018-0476-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0476-5))
- **C1148.** The Sapelo Island analysis argues that colorblind sea-level-rise adaptation can reproduce environmental racism when it ignores racialized land ownership, employment and barriers to African American participation. *Regime: Racial coastal formation: The environmental injustice of colorblind adaptation planning for sea-level rise.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Dean Hardy 2017, [doi:10.1016/j.geoforum.2017.10.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geoforum.2017.10.005))
- **C1149.** At Grenville Bay, six decades of evidence and wave modeling link reef degradation more strongly than historical climate or sea-level change to northern-bay erosion and motivate an engineered reef-restoration pilot for risk reduction. *Regime: Coral reefs for coastal protection: A new methodological approach and engineering case study in Grenada.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Reguero 2018, [doi:10.1016/j.jenvman.2018.01.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2018.01.024))
- **C1150.** A review of Indonesian community adaptation identifies infrastructure, mangrove rehabilitation, warning systems, education, livelihood diversification, resettlement, drainage and resource management, emphasizing participatory and locally flexible implementation. *Regime: Adaptation Strategies of Coastal Communities to Sea Level Rise Due to Climate Change.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Kamakaula 2024, [doi:10.58812/wsshs.v2i07.1045](https://doi.org/10.58812/wsshs.v2i07.1045))
