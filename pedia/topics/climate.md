# Sea-level rise and climate adaptation

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Climate-driven mean sea-level rise shifts the baseline on which tides, waves, surge and river or rainfall flooding act. Small mean changes can strongly amplify threshold exceedance, while exposure, vulnerability, geomorphic response, ecosystems and adaptation determine consequences.

**Governing physics.** Ocean thermal expansion and land-ice loss raise mean sea level; regional circulation, gravity, vertical land motion and tides alter local change. Storms, waves, runoff, erosion, sediment accretion, wetland migration and reef condition govern how that baseline becomes flooding or shoreline impact.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Useful controls include sea-level increment relative to local extreme-level scale, flood-frequency amplification, exposed-population fraction, vulnerability indices normalized to 0–1, accommodation-space ratio, defense freeboard relative to projected total water level and adaptation benefit-cost or avoided-loss ratios.

**Major equations.** Assessment combines sea-level projections with extreme-value or joint-probability distributions, dynamic inundation and erosion models, exposure overlays and risk as a function of hazard, exposure and vulnerability. Carbon accounts quantify stored stock and avoided emissions; adaptation comparisons modify hazards, assets or response capacity.

**Typical methods.** Workflows combine tide gauges, altimetry, elevation and socioeconomic data with emissions and sea-level scenarios, tide-surge-wave or compound-flood models, dynamic shoreline response, ecosystem and carbon inventories, vulnerability indices and participatory adaptation assessment. Uncertainty should be propagated across all layers.

**Numerical models.** Models range from probabilistic sea-level and extreme-value ensembles to global or local hydrodynamic flooding, GIS exposure and vulnerability overlays, wetland accommodation models, shoreline equilibrium or erosion models and coupled demographic scenarios. Static bathtub mapping omits important dynamic pathways.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence includes global coastal populations and elevation models, probabilistic extreme-level projections, national US 30 m compound-flood maps, Mediterranean heritage sites, California dynamic exposure, Bangladesh cyclone records, Australian blue-carbon inventories and Grenada reef and shoreline observations.

**Validated ranges.** Reported evidence spans site, city, national and global scales and scenarios through 2100. Quantitative values are conditional on emissions, elevation, population, protection and process assumptions; the US compound model reports map-validation CSI of 0.69–0.82, not universal predictive skill.

**Recent advances.** Recent advances correct coastal elevation with machine learning, couple waves, surge, tides, rainfall and rivers, represent erosion and cliff retreat dynamically, quantify blue carbon and wetland accommodation, and compare flexible adaptation pathways with explicit equity and ecosystem services.

**Disagreements.** Magnitude and timing differ across emissions, ice-sheet, vertical-motion, storm, wave, demographic and adaptation assumptions. Static exposure can seriously understate dynamic flooding, while unconstrained global assessments can overstate realized impacts if protection or migration is ignored.

**Limitations.** Elevation bias, incomplete hazard dependence, coarse coastal grids, uncertain ice response, socioeconomic pathways, autonomous adaptation, protection failure, ecosystem feedbacks and distributional inequity limit inference. Global rankings identify hotspots but do not replace local design analysis.

**Open questions.** Priorities include local vertical-motion integration, joint compound extremes, dynamic morphology, cascading infrastructure failure, ecosystem adaptation limits, migration and development feedbacks, equitable financing, adaptation effectiveness and transparent deep-uncertainty decisions.

**Seminal papers.** Detection and attribution established climate-driven sea-level rise; scenario and extreme-value studies translated it into changing flood frequency, and integrated vulnerability frameworks joined physical exposure with social capacity. Dynamic and nature-based approaches subsequently broadened assessment beyond static inundation.
