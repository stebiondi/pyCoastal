# Seagrass and aquatic vegetation

`ecohydraulics.seagrass` | Canopy-flow and sediment feedbacks.

Parent: [Coastal ecosystems and ecohydraulics](ecohydraulics.md)

Papers: 53. Claims: 50. Equations: 0.

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Seagrass modifies waves, currents, turbulence and sediment through distributed drag, flexible reconfiguration, canopy-interface shear, wave streaming, near-bed sheltering, and particle interception; these physical changes feed back on light, accretion and meadow persistence.

**Governing physics.** Relative blade–water velocity sets drag; flexibility reduces projected area and changes phase; cumulative drag dissipates waves; canopy shear generates coherent vortices and vertical exchange; sheltering lowers bed stress while wakes add turbulence; sediment–light feedback can create bistability.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include submergence, canopy height/depth, wave excursion/shoot spacing, Cauchy number, blade stiffness and buoyancy, Reynolds and Keulegan–Carpenter numbers, frontal-area density, meadow length/wavelength, gap/patch size, current/wave velocity ratio, and Shields stress.

**Major equations.** Models use Morison/drag force with effective frontal area and relative velocity, beam or blade dynamics, wave-energy decay, Reynolds-averaged canopy momentum, turbulent kinetic-energy budgets, advection–diffusion–settling, critical Shields stress, and coupled sediment–light–biomass equations.

**Typical methods.** Methods span wave/current flumes with flexible mimics or live plants, ADV velocity and turbulence, wave gauges, force and blade-motion imaging, PIV, sediment traps and optical backscatter, field meadow transects and storms, spectral budgets, coupled CFD–structure models, and ecosystem feedback models.

**Numerical models.** Models include relative-velocity blade dynamics, eelgrass–flow coupling, meadow-scale attenuation, digital twins for surge scenarios, subgrid canopy closures, SPH–finite-element stem interaction, sediment–light bistability, and spatial particle-deposition models.

**Experimental datasets.** The evidence includes Posidonia and Zostera field meadows, natural and transplanted canopies, storm before/after surveys, oscillatory and combined-flow flumes, flexible/stiff mimics, fragmented and gapped patches, particle capture/deposition, streaming observations, and spectral wave dissipation.

**Validated ranges.** The corpus covers low-energy through storm waves, steady, oscillatory and combined flow, submerged and near-emergent canopies, rigid-to-flexible blades, single- and multispecies meadows, intact-to-fragmented patches, and blade-to-barrier-coast scales; detailed numeric ranges remain source-specific.

**Recent advances.** Recent work directly measures frequency-dependent meadow dissipation, combined-flow resuspension thresholds, turbulence and bedload, carbon-accretion heterogeneity, storm-surge digital twins, transplanted-meadow attenuation, and stem-scale SPH–FE interaction.

**Disagreements.** Rigid-canopy drag can overpredict attenuation when blades move with water, yet flexible canopies may generate different turbulence rather than uniformly less effect; vegetation can both shelter the bed and add wake stress; shoot density alone is insufficient when flexibility, morphology, patch length and gaps vary.

**Limitations.** Mimic materials and geometric scaling distort reconfiguration; many studies isolate waves or currents; field morphology is seasonal and heterogeneous; storm breakage, cohesive sediment, root effects, species mixtures, meadow recovery, and full-scale sediment budgets remain underrepresented.

**Open questions.** Priorities include spectral dissipation across changing morphology, combined wave–current–tide forcing, patch-edge networks, breakage and uprooting, cohesive sediment and carbon, sediment–light thresholds, restoration trajectories, and uncertainty-aware upscaling to coastal protection.

**Seminal papers.** The branch progresses from eelgrass flow and transport observations and relative-blade-velocity drag to flexible blade models, canopy vortex and streaming measurements, fragmented-patch hydraulics, sediment feedback, spectral budgets, and coupled fluid–structure simulation.
