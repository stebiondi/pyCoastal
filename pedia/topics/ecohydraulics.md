# Coastal ecosystems and ecohydraulics

`ecohydraulics` | Coupled hydrodynamic and ecological processes.

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Coastal organisms alter waves, currents, turbulence and sediment transport, while hydroperiod, salinity, bed change and disturbance control organism survival and morphology. These two-way feedbacks create habitat and can provide flood, erosion, water-quality and carbon benefits.

**Governing physics.** Vegetation drag extracts wave and current momentum; flexibility, buoyancy and reconfiguration change drag. Canopy shear layers and wakes redistribute turbulence and sediment, roots reinforce soil, and tidal inundation controls oxygen, salinity and habitat zonation. Morphodynamic and ecological responses feed back on flow geometry.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include Reynolds and Froude numbers, Keulegan–Carpenter number, submergence, frontal-area density, solid-volume fraction, Cauchy or flexibility number, wave transmission and attenuation coefficients, Shields stress, Rouse number, hydroperiod fraction and relative sea-level/accretion rate.

**Major equations.** Models couple mass and momentum conservation to vegetation drag, wave-energy dissipation, turbulent diffusivity, sediment advection–diffusion and erosion/deposition, then link inundation or stress to habitat response. Drag and bulk dissipation closures must retain the scale and morphology assumptions of their calibration.

**Typical methods.** Evidence combines flumes with live or surrogate plants, field velocity/wave/sediment and vegetation surveys, erosion tests, remote sensing, calibrated hydrodynamic or morphodynamic models and before–after restoration monitoring. Comparative designs vary density, height, flexibility, patch geometry and water level.

**Numerical models.** Approaches range from cylinder-array drag and diffusivity closures to phase-averaged wave–vegetation, 2D wetland hydrodynamics, inlet circulation and coupled hydro-ecological or morphodynamic models. High-resolution ecological detail is usually limited to small domains; landscape models require aggregation.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed datasets include a 410 ha controlled-tide Ramsar restoration monitored for eight years, real-morphology Typha channel profiles, 1:20 marsh–dyke basin tests, winter marsh erosion flumes, Phragmites wave tests, an Oregon Inlet model and in-situ high-velocity marsh erodibility tests.

**Validated ranges.** Findings are regime-specific: artificial Phragmites reduced wave height 6–25% at canopy entry; marsh erodibility tests exceeded 0.5 m/s; the restoration covered 410 ha. These values cannot be transferred across species, season, scale or geometry without similarity and uncertainty checks.

**Recent advances.** Recent work uses real plant morphology, automated eco-hydrological restoration, in-situ failure testing, cross-disciplinary vegetation taxonomies, gray-to-green system comparisons and nested high-resolution/landscape models with more explicit ecological variability.

**Disagreements.** Aboveground biomass can strongly attenuate waves yet showed no mean winter erosion benefit in one soil-controlled test, indicating that wave attenuation, bed reinforcement and breach erosion are distinct services. Gray–green comparisons also depend on whether success means local velocity reduction, system change or ecological value.

**Limitations.** Surrogate fidelity, scale effects, plant reconfiguration, seasonal biomass, mortality, patch fragmentation, uncertain drag, limited extreme-event data, evolving topography and mismatch between patch observations and landscape decisions constrain prediction.

**Open questions.** Priorities include multiscale upscaling, mixed-species and fragmented canopies, failure thresholds under extremes, long-term adaptation to sea-level rise, sediment–vegetation feedback, standardized attenuation metrics, ecological trade-offs and monitored performance of hybrid defenses.

**Seminal papers.** Foundational canopy-flow and vegetation-drag studies established momentum extraction and wake turbulence; wetland biogeomorphology connected inundation and sediment accretion to habitat persistence. Nature-based engineering later framed these processes as quantifiable protection services.

## Claims

- **C1158.** Ecohydraulic prediction and management require joint physical and biological treatment across plant, patch, gap and landscape scales rather than one-way use of ecology as a model input. *Regime: Fragmented marine canopies across multiple habitat types..* [literature_review_statement, review] (Andrew M. Folkard 2019, [doi:10.3389/fmars.2019.00279](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmars.2019.00279))
- **C1586.** Coastal eutrophication response is filtered by system-specific attributes and includes linked changes in oxygen, water clarity, vegetation, biogeochemistry, harmful blooms and food webs rather than a universal nutrient-to-biomass response. *Regime: Our evolving conceptual model of the coastal eutrophication problem.* [literature_review_statement, review] (James E. Cloern 2001, [doi:10.3354/meps210223](https://doi.org/10.3354/meps210223))
- **C1597.** A systematic review of 95 papers finds hybrid sea defenses potentially combine robustness and ecological benefit but lack sufficiently consistent quantitative wave-structure performance evidence for confident transfer. *Regime: Advances in understanding the challenges and opportunities of hybrid sea defence approaches for coastal resilience.* [literature_review_statement, review] (Xihang Xu 2025, [doi:10.1016/j.envc.2025.101130](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envc.2025.101130))
