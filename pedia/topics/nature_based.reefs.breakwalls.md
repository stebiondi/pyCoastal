# Living shoreline breakwalls

`nature_based.reefs.breakwalls` | Porous brush, branch, reef, and modular barriers used to attenuate waves while retaining ecological connectivity.

## Synthesis

**Well established.** The reviewed branch-and-post living breakwalls reduced boat-wake energy, but attenuation depended strongly on design and tidal depth rather than following a single transferable transmission rate.

**Governing physics.** For the lower-porosity design, transmission rose with tidal depth most clearly for relative structure height hs/h approximately 0.5-1; near emergence, breaking and bottom friction added dissipation absent from the porous-only theory.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include porosity, relative structure height and submergence, width relative to wavelength, transmitted-to-incident energy, water depth relative to crest, wake steepness, shoreline-change rate normalized by control retreat and maintenance interval relative to material lifetime.

**Major equations.** Performance is described by wave-energy transmission and reflection ratios, energy-flux balances, porous resistance and shoreline-position or retreat-rate comparisons between treatment and control. Long-term benefit additionally requires a service-life and maintenance balance for biodegradable fill and stable supports.

**Typical methods.** Studies deploy incident and lee wave sensors across tides and boat wakes, characterize porosity and geometry, pair protected and reference marsh margins, repeat shoreline surveys over years, track oyster or vegetation response and document repair frequency, component loss and functional lifetime.

**Numerical models.** A calibrated porous-transmission formulation represents branch resistance but omits some external breaking and bottom friction near emergence. The shoreline study uses treatment-control field comparison rather than a predictive morphodynamic model.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence comprises 290 boat wakes at bundled and unbundled branch breakwalls and 2015-2019 monitoring at three energetic northeast Florida shorelines with brush breakwalls, oyster gabions and paired controls.

**Validated ranges.** The bundled n=0.7 design transmitted mostly 9%-70% of incident wake energy and averaged 53% during the GTM deployment. The unbundled n=0.9 design transmitted more than 70% of energy for 84% of wakes and averaged 83% during the North Peninsula deployment.

**Recent advances.** Recent advances combine modular biodegradable fills, oyster gabions and hybrid sills, field spectral monitoring, UAV shoreline and habitat mapping, life-cycle and maintenance accounting, adaptive replacement and coupled hydrodynamic–ecological performance metrics.

**Disagreements.** Greater material density and lower porosity can improve attenuation but increase cost, ecological footprint and failure load; biodegradable components support habitat goals yet require replacement. Short-term wave reduction does not guarantee multi-year shoreline benefit, and shoreline stability does not isolate each component contribution.

**Limitations.** The observed 0.7-versus-0.9 porosity contrast is not a controlled porosity experiment because branch bundling, field site, and tidal range changed simultaneously. Biodegradable branch fill required recurring maintenance and lasted roughly one year, whereas the supporting posts persisted longer; hydraulic performance alone is therefore insufficient for lifecycle design.

**Open questions.** Controlled multi-site evidence is needed to separate porosity, material, geometry, tides, breaking, sediment response, ecological performance, durability, storms, and maintenance cost.

**Seminal papers.** Permeable breakwater and brush-fence studies established porous attenuation, while oyster-reef and living-shoreline experiments extended performance to ecological structures and paired shoreline controls, reframing maintenance and biological development as design variables.

## Claims

- **C34.** For the bundled-branch breakwall with estimated porosity 0.7, observed wave-energy transmission was mostly 9%-70% and averaged 53%. *Regime: GTM branch-and-post design, approximately 4.3 by 0.6 by 0.55 m, boat wakes and tides observed in March-April 2018..* [direct_finding, field] (Safak 2020, [doi:10.1016/j.csr.2020.104268](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csr.2020.104268))
- **C35.** For the unbundled-branch breakwall with estimated porosity 0.9, energy transmission exceeded 70% in 84% of wake cases and averaged 83%. *Regime: NP branch-and-post design, approximately 4.3 by 0.6 by 0.55 m, boat wakes and smaller tidal range observed in April-May 2019..* [direct_finding, field] (Safak 2020, [doi:10.1016/j.csr.2020.104268](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csr.2020.104268))
- **C40.** The branch fill required maintenance every four to six months at GTM and had an estimated functional life of 12-18 months, while unbundled NP fill lasted less than one year; fence posts remained stable after three years. *Regime: Crepe-myrtle bundled fill at GTM and eastern-cedar unbundled fill at NP under local bioerosion and vessel-wake exposure..* [direct_finding, field] (Safak 2020, [doi:10.1016/j.csr.2020.104268](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csr.2020.104268))
- **C181.** From 2015-2019 at three wake-exposed northeast Florida sites, marsh margins protected by brush breakwalls and oyster gabions were stable or advanced about 1 m/y, whereas paired controls retreated at rates approaching 2 m/y. *Regime: Vessel-wake-dominated estuarine marshes with 1-1.5 m semidiurnal tide and local oyster recruitment..* [direct_finding, field] (Safak 2020, [doi:10.1016/j.ecoleng.2020.106071](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoleng.2020.106071))
- **C1191.** Replicated oyster-shell breakwater reefs reduced shoreline retreat by more than 40% at one site and enhanced several fish and invertebrate groups, but loss of reef relief compromised protection and erosion remained high. *Regime: Oyster Reefs as Natural Breakwaters Mitigate Shoreline Loss and Facilitate Fisheries.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Steven B. Scyphers 2011, [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0022396](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0022396))
- **C1197.** At Kutubdia Island, oyster breakwaters attenuated 0.1–0.5 m waves, accumulated 29 cm of lee-side sediment, reduced erosion 54% relative to controls and facilitated seaward saltmarsh expansion. *Regime: Oyster breakwater reefs promote adjacent mudflat stability and salt marsh growth in a monsoon dominated subtropical coast.* [direct_finding, mixed] (M. Shah Nawaz Chowdhury 2019, [doi:10.1038/s41598-019-44925-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-44925-6))
- **C1199.** Across US oyster living shorelines, reefs inundated under 50% of the time attenuated wave height by 68% but were unsuitable oyster habitat, whereas suitable reefs above 50% inundation attenuated only about 5%, exposing an ecological–engineering design trade-off. *Regime: Large‐scale variation in wave attenuation of oyster reef living shorelines and the influence of inundation duration.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Rebecca L. Morris 2021, [doi:10.1002/eap.2382](https://doi.org/10.1002/eap.2382))
- **C1203.** Validated FUNWAVE-TVD simulations indicate oyster-reef attenuation depends on waves and depth and can remain sustainable under sea-level rise only with viable growth habitat; modeled maximum capacity required about nine years after construction. *Regime: Assessing Wave Attenuation With Rising Sea Levels for Sustainable Oyster Reef-Based Living Shorelines.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Reza Salatin 2022, [doi:10.3389/fbuil.2022.884849](https://doi.org/10.3389/fbuil.2022.884849))
- **C1204.** Seven-year Gandy's Beach monitoring found constructed oyster reefs met most habitat objectives but none of the material-stability, wave-attenuation, or sediment-elevation protection objectives fully, demonstrating the need for goal-specific long-term monitoring. *Regime: Seven Years of Monitoring the Development of an Oyster Reef Living Shoreline.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Jenny P. Shinn 2025, [doi:10.1007/s12237-024-01437-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12237-024-01437-5))
