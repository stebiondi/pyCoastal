# Loads within building arrays

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Within the reviewed experiment, upstream structures shielded downstream buildings from peak breaking-wave pressure, but the amount is configuration- and regime-specific rather than a universal reduction factor.

**Governing physics.** Breaking location and structural setback govern impulsive loading: the largest pressures occurred when the wave broke on or immediately before the specimen.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include Froude number, relative wave height, structure width and spacing relative to depth, blockage ratio, setback relative to breaking length, row and lateral position, inundation depth relative to building height, pressure coefficient and peak-to-hydrostatic pressure ratio.

**Major equations.** Array loading is assessed through pressure integration and force or moment balance, with hydrostatic, drag, momentum-flux and impulsive components. Design equations relate pressure to inundation depth or velocity, while blockage and shielding modify the local depth, velocity and breaking state used as inputs.

**Typical methods.** Studies generate controlled bores or long waves over a 1:20 bathymetry, vary breaking location and structure arrangement, measure free surface and high-rate pressures on multiple faces and rows, integrate loads, compare isolated and array cases and audit design equations using locally disturbed rather than bare-earth hydraulics.

**Numerical models.** The current evidence evaluates empirical Japanese design equations against physical-array pressures. Numerical shallow-water, nonhydrostatic or CFD array models are not yet reviewed for this node and require validation against local depth, velocity, pressure and impulse together.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence is one idealized 1:20 physical model of a seaside building array under breaking and nonbreaking tsunami-like waves, resolving upstream shielding, local breaking position and comparison with Japanese Cabinet Office pressure equations.

**Validated ranges.** The reported 40%-70% peak-pressure reduction applies only to the study's 1:20 idealized array under breaking-wave trials; the accessible abstract does not expose the exact wave, spacing, or setback ranges.

**Recent advances.** Recent advances use dense pressure arrays, instrumented urban layouts, high-resolution nonhydrostatic and CFD solvers, debris and buoyancy coupling, probabilistic urban configurations and multiscale workflows connecting regional inundation to local structural demand.

**Disagreements.** Upstream buildings can shield downstream faces yet increase blockage, flow channelization, lateral loads or local water levels. Bare-earth hazard models may support regional inundation but can be nonconservative for structure pressure when the built array changes breaking and hydraulics.

**Limitations.** Bare-earth water levels can be nonconservative inputs for breaking-wave pressure design because the building array changes local inundation and impact conditions.

**Open questions.** Full-text extraction and additional arrays are needed to resolve spacing, setback, blockage, breaking location, three-dimensional flow, and scale effects before deriving transferable shielding factors.

**Seminal papers.** Tsunami force formulations began with hydrostatic and drag analogies and bore-impact experiments; post-2004 and 2011 surveys motivated building-specific pressure guidance, while later flume arrays exposed shielding, blockage and local-breaking effects.
