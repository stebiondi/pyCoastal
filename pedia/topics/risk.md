## Synthesis

**Well established.** Coastal risk arises from the joint occurrence of hazard, exposed people/assets/ecosystems, vulnerability or fragility, and consequences. Changes in any component can dominate risk, and correlated or sequential hazards can amplify impacts beyond independent single-hazard estimates.

**Governing physics.** Storms, waves, surge, rainfall, rivers, erosion, tsunami and defense failure generate loads and inundation; receptor geometry, materials, social capacity and networks govern damage and recovery. Climate, development and adaptation make hazard, exposure and vulnerability nonstationary.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include annual exceedance probability, return-period-to-lifetime ratio, conditional failure probability, damage ratio, vulnerability index, protection reliability, hazard and loss correlation, benefit-cost ratio, expected annual loss relative to GDP or budget and recovery-time ratios.

**Major equations.** Expected risk integrates consequence over hazard intensity and probability, often as expected annual damage. Fragility gives conditional failure probability; vulnerability maps intensity to damage ratio or sensitivity; reliability compares stochastic load and resistance, and multihazard models represent joint, conditional or cascading events.

**Typical methods.** Workflows define system and receptors, construct probabilistic hazards, map exposure, estimate fragility/vulnerability and losses, model dependence and cascades, validate each component, aggregate uncertainty and compare mitigation. Participatory and distributional analysis complements physical-economic metrics.

**Numerical models.** Models include extreme-value and stochastic-event sets, copulas and joint probabilities, hydraulic and tsunami simulations, Monte Carlo reliability, fragility and damage functions, GIS exposure/vulnerability overlays, network disruption and scenario-based future risk.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence includes global roads/railways, WorldRiskIndex and INFORM outcomes, Po flood and levee scenarios, British bridge failures, 726 Tohoku source realizations, Catalan beach sectors, masonry flood cases and Bangladesh cyclonic-flood literature and census indicators.

**Validated ranges.** Reported values are context-specific: global transport EAD spans $3.1–22 billion, high-vulnerability countries show 15-fold event mortality, and 726 tsunami sources represent one Tohoku case. Such values cannot be transferred without harmonized exposure, vulnerability and uncertainty.

**Recent advances.** Recent work uses stochastic source ensembles, spatial multihazard interaction, dynamic exposure and vulnerability scenarios, global infrastructure networks, uncertainty visualization and impact chains that connect climate drivers to physical and socioeconomic consequences.

**Disagreements.** Composite indices improve comparability but can obscure causal mechanisms and weighting; detailed fragility models are mechanistic but data intensive. Expected annual loss supports portfolios yet can hide tail, distributional and nonmarket consequences. Static rankings may reverse under future development or adaptation.

**Limitations.** Sparse loss and failure data, reporting bias, uncertain extremes, dependence, defense behavior, indirect and cascading effects, informal assets, social inequity, model resolution and future scenarios limit confidence. Validation of hazard alone does not validate risk.

**Open questions.** Priorities include compound and cascading dependence, dynamic vulnerability and recovery, indirect network losses, ecosystem and cultural consequences, equity, behavioral adaptation, deep uncertainty, transferable fragility and transparent validation of composite indices.

**Seminal papers.** Risk theory formalized probability–consequence integration; engineering reliability introduced load–resistance and fragility functions; hazard mapping and vulnerability research then combined spatial exposure with physical and social susceptibility. Disaster-risk frameworks broadened analysis to resilience and governance.

## Claims

- **C1221.** Coastal risk assessment should integrate probabilistic hazard, exposure, vulnerability and consequences across present and future conditions while borrowing validated methods across hazard communities only with explicit transfer assumptions. *Regime: Global and regional natural-hazard risk assessment including floods and tsunamis..* [literature_review_statement, review] (Philip J. Ward 2020, [doi:10.5194/nhess-20-1069-2020](https://doi.org/10.5194/nhess-20-1069-2020))
- **C1735.** Comparative evidence from Hong Kong and Singapore indicates that resilient coastal-city flood management requires integrating flood-risk and climate-adaptation policy into spatial planning, resolving agency responsibilities, and coupling engineered controls with long-term inter-agency governance. *Regime: Dense Asian coastal cities exposed to pluvial, fluvial, tidal, storm-surge, and sea-level risks under continued urbanization and climate change..* [literature_review_statement, review] (Faith Ka Shun Chan 2018, [doi:10.1016/j.jclepro.2018.03.217](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2018.03.217))
