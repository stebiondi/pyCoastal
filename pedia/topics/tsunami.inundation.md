## Synthesis

**Well established.** Explicit wetting-and-drying models can represent two-dimensional tsunami inundation rather than inferring flooded area solely from water-level superelevation or cross-sectional runup; the reviewed evidence here is one demonstrated model application, not a comparative consensus. Across the two reviewed model studies, credible inundation use requires explicit moving wet/dry shorelines and validation against analytical or laboratory runup benchmarks; successful propagation alone is not sufficient evidence of inundation accuracy. Tsunami-model evaluation distinguishes validation of the numerical solution against the governing equations from verification against geophysical observations; both are required for defensible inundation use. Tsunami inundation prediction requires a moving wet/dry shoreline, terrain-resolved mass and momentum transfer, and validation of inland extent, depth or velocity; offshore propagation agreement alone does not establish onshore accuracy.

**Governing physics.** The reviewed model links source displacement, nonlinear dispersive propagation, breaking, runup/rundown, and inland wetting through a higher-order Boussinesq framework with surface-roller breaking and dry-bed boundaries. Inundation couples long-wave shoaling and runup with nonlinear advection, gravity, bed and land-cover friction, wetting and drying, breaking or bore motion, terrain channeling, and interactions with buildings and defenses.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Validation regimes were characterized by relative solitary-wave height H/d and normalized runup R/H; the conical-island cases tested H/d=0.10 and 0.20. Relevant controls include relative wave height and runup, Froude number, slope, relative roughness and blockage, grid-to-feature scale, wet/dry depth tolerance, Reynolds effects in physical or RANS models, and nondimensional arrival, depth, velocity and force errors.

**Major equations.** The formulation uses higher-order Boussinesq continuity and momentum equations, with a bottom-displacement time derivative available for active generation and alternative passive free-surface displacement used in the regional scenarios. Most detailed models solve depth-integrated nonlinear shallow-water conservation equations, while higher-fidelity RANS resolves vertical structure; reduced methods use Bernoulli energy and friction loss, Green-law transformations, response functions, or other calibrated approximations.

**Typical methods.** The demonstrated workflow combines laboratory runup validation, regional bathymetry, coastal terrain, empirical earthquake-source dimensions, passive tsunami generation, phase-resolving propagation, and dry-bed inundation mapping. A defensible evaluation sequence combines basic hydrodynamic checks and grid convergence with analytical, laboratory, and field benchmarks, followed by scientific review and application-specific operational testing. Workflows combine a source scenario, nested bathymetry and topography, stable wetting/drying, spatial friction or explicit structures, grid and parameter checks, comparison with laboratory or post-event observations, and mapped extent, depth, velocity, arrival time or load outputs.

**Numerical models.** The reviewed higher-order Boussinesq model reproduced the main plane-beach runup sequence and showed closer conical-island agreement for H/d=0.10 than H/d=0.20, where front-side runup was underpredicted. The reviewed models span different physics: UBO-TSUFD solves nondispersive nonlinear shallow-water equations with Manning friction, whereas the Eastern Mediterranean model uses higher-order Boussinesq dispersion and a surface-roller breaking treatment. The evidence spans COMCOT, GeoClaw, depth-integrated shallow-water solvers, three-dimensional RANS, SWEpy GPU finite volumes, nested linear-to-local models, probabilistic response-function inversion, and rapid energy-conservation approximations.

**Experimental datasets.** Two independent laboratory benchmarks were used: solitary-wave runup/rundown on a 1:19.85 plane beach and directional maximum runup around a 14-degree conical island. The reviewed laboratory benchmark suite spans a 1:19.85 plane beach, a composite beach, a conical island, a complex three-dimensional beach, and three-dimensional landslide generation and runup. The expanded evidence includes 2004 Thailand and Banda Aceh field information, 85 surveyed tsunami-pole flow depths, Onagawa eyewitness video and damage observations, and synthetic or historical runup records used for inversion and validation.

**Validated ranges.** Laboratory validation in the reviewed source covers a 1:19.85 plane beach at 0.2 m depth and a conical island at 0.32 m depth with H/d=0.10 and 0.20; it does not constitute observed validation of the Eastern Mediterranean inundation maps. Validation remains benchmark-specific: UBO-TSUFD covers four analytical/laboratory tests including a pre-breaking Monai comparison through 30 s, while the higher-order Boussinesq model covers a 1:19.85 plane beach and a 14-degree conical island at H/d=0.10 and 0.20. The canonical 1:19.85 plane-beach dataset contains more than 40 solitary-wave tests over 6.25-38.32 cm depth and H/d=0.021-0.626; its reported breaking threshold H/d>0.045 is beach-specific and should not be generalized to other slopes or waveforms. Reported validation is case-specific, covering selected historical events, local terrain, source scenarios, benchmark inflows and building layouts; accuracy and cost findings cannot be transferred without checking resolution, roughness, wet/dry treatment and forcing regime.

**Recent advances.** For the two paper-specific 1 m source-amplitude scenarios, modeled inundated area was 3.429 km2 at the Crete site and 0.641 km2 at the Sicily site; steeper Sicily terrain was associated with a narrower inundation zone. Scenario outputs demonstrate strong site dependence rather than transferable constants: reported penetration is about 100 m at La Plaia and 500 m near the Simeto mouth in the corrected Catania scenario, while separate one-meter-source cases report 3.429 km2 and 0.641 km2 inundation at selected Crete and Sicily sites. Recent advances combine probabilistic runup inversion, rapid physics-based screening between bathtub and full models, semi-empirical operational chains, detailed 2-D/3-D intercomparison, terrain and defense sensitivity, and open GPU-accelerated unstructured solvers.

**Disagreements.** An internal reporting inconsistency remains in the plane-beach benchmark: the Figure 1 caption states H/d=0.25 while the validation prose states H/d=0.28. Published-record corrections and inconsistencies must be carried with model benchmarks: the UBO-TSUFD source caption was formally corrected to M0=6.41e21 Nm and Mw=7.17, while the Boussinesq paper reports its plane-beach H/d as 0.25 in a caption and 0.28 in prose. Model fidelity is decision-dependent: explicit three-dimensional flow can improve local force representation but is costly, while depth-integrated and reduced methods support larger ensembles or rapid mapping but simplify vertical structure, breaking and obstacle-scale flow.

**Limitations.** The regional results are representative numerical scenarios, not historical reconstructions or observed inundation validation, and must not be linearly rescaled to other initial amplitudes because the simulated processes are nonlinear. Neither reviewed Mediterranean application provides observed validation of its regional inundation map: the Catania case uses modern geometry and a speculative corrected source, while the Crete/Sicily cases are representative one-meter source scenarios that cannot be linearly rescaled. Benchmark success reduces uncertainty but does not guarantee accurate prediction for every tsunami; one comparison is insufficient and extrapolation remains limited by source ambiguity, bathymetry, breaking, friction treatment, and benchmark coverage. Ad hoc friction coefficients used mainly as numerical stabilizers have no assured predictive reliability outside the configurations in which the model was tested. Dominant limitations are uncertain tsunami sources, elevation and bathymetry error, unresolved buildings and defenses, empirical roughness, sparse inland velocity and force observations, numerical wet/dry sensitivity, high computational cost, and validation confined to a few events or benchmarks.

**Open questions.** Needed evidence includes real-event inundation validation in the Eastern Mediterranean, sensitivity to uncertain source geometry and terrain resolution, and uncertainty propagation into operational hazard maps. Comparative external validation is needed to determine when nondispersive shallow-water versus dispersive Boussinesq physics materially changes inundation predictions, especially where breaking, uncertain sources, harbor geometry, and high-resolution terrain interact. Model intercomparison under identical emergency conditions remains necessary to establish relative speed, accuracy, and reliability rather than inferring superiority from separate benchmark demonstrations. Shared questions concern transferable roughness, coupled source-to-inundation uncertainty, representation of buildings and defenses, sediment and debris feedback, validation of velocity and loads, probabilistic maps, compound boundaries, and reliable real-time computation.

**Seminal papers.** Foundational inundation practice grew from nonlinear shallow-water runup theory, analytical moving-shoreline solutions, laboratory plane-beach and island benchmarks, finite-difference and finite-volume wetting/drying algorithms, and systematic post-tsunami field surveys.

## Equations

### Fully reflecting coastal boundary

$$
\frac{\partial \zeta}{\partial n}=0,\quad U_n=0
$$

Regime: Solid fully reflecting coast option; inundation simulations used the dry-bed boundary condition.

Variables: `n` landward unit normal; `U_n` normal depth-averaged velocity

Source: (Samaras 2015, [doi:10.5194/os-11-643-2015](https://doi.org/10.5194/os-11-643-2015))

### Long-wave surf similarity

$$
\xi=\sqrt{\pi}(a_0/h_0)^{-1/2}(\Omega_1^2 h_0/(g\tan^2\beta))^{-1/2}
$$

Regime: Single sinusoidal long-wave runup on a uniform plane beach.

Variables: `xi` surf-similarity parameter; `a0` offshore amplitude; `h0` still-water depth; `Omega1` effective angular duration; `g` gravitational acceleration; `beta` beach angle

Source: (Goseberg 2013, [doi:10.5194/nhess-13-2991-2013](https://doi.org/10.5194/nhess-13-2991-2013))
