# Relative sea level and subsidence

`climate.subsidence` | Vertical land motion contributions.

Parent: [Sea-level rise and climate adaptation](climate.md)

Papers: 9. Claims: 8. Equations: 0.

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Relative sea-level change combines ocean-surface change with vertical land motion; subsidence can equal or exceed the climatic ocean component and therefore must be estimated locally.

**Governing physics.** Coastal land moves through groundwater and hydrocarbon withdrawal, sediment compaction, loading, tectonic and postseismic deformation, glacial isostatic adjustment and shallow infrastructure settlement, while steric and mass changes move the sea surface.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Useful normalized controls include subsidence-to-eustatic-rate ratio, vertical-motion uncertainty relative to trend, record duration relative to climate oscillations, compaction relative to sediment thickness and projected subsidence relative to defense freeboard.

**Major equations.** With upward-positive land motion, relative sea-level rate is approximately absolute sea-level rate minus vertical land-motion rate; time-varying analyses add acceleration, periodic variability, datum offsets and correlated error terms.

**Typical methods.** Studies combine long tide-gauge records with colocated GNSS, satellite altimetry or InSAR, homogenize datum and offsets, estimate nonlinear trends and accelerations, compare independent geodetic products and attribute mechanisms using extraction, geology and earthquake histories.

**Numerical models.** Methods range from segmented and nonlinear tide-gauge regressions to differencing relative tide-gauge and absolute altimetry signals; hydromechanical aquifer or sediment-compaction models can test causal attribution but are not represented directly in this reviewed set.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence covers Galveston Pier 21 since 1909, five long-record Oceania gauges plus Tuvalu and Adelaide cases, incomplete Chinese gauge records, and 50 Southeast Asian sites with overlapping 1993–2020 tide-gauge and altimetry observations.

**Validated ranges.** Evidence spans individual gauges and regional networks over multi-decadal periods. Rates are site- and interval-specific and should not be transferred without colocated vertical-motion observations and consistent reference frames.

**Recent advances.** Recent work fuses tide gauges, GNSS, altimetry and InSAR, resolves nonlinear site histories, maps aquifer and sediment compaction, and incorporates time-varying vertical motion into local sea-level and flood-risk projections.

**Disagreements.** Linear trends can obscure changing extraction, postseismic transients and compaction, while short records can misidentify climate variability as acceleration. Different geodetic products and reference frames can yield conflicting vertical-motion estimates.

**Limitations.** Sparse colocated GNSS, datum changes, gauge relocation, altimetry coastal contamination, InSAR reference choices, nonlinear motion, short records and uncertain causal attribution limit projections; abstract-level values also require full-text methodological audit.

**Open questions.** Priorities include forecasting nonlinear subsidence under changing water management, separating shallow from deep motion, reconciling geodetic frames, propagating local motion into probabilistic extremes and sustaining dense observations in rapidly subsiding deltas and cities.

**Seminal papers.** Tide-gauge geodesy established that relative records combine ocean and crustal motion; leveling and later continuous GNSS enabled correction, while satellite altimetry and InSAR expanded regional separation and spatial mapping.

## Claims

- **C144.** A 27-year tide-gauge and satellite-altimetry dataset at 50 Southeast Asian coastal sites revealed highly variable, nonlinear land motion linked to groundwater extraction, postseismic deformation, and sediment compaction. *Regime: The 50 sites with overlapping quality-controlled records; attribution strength varies by location..* [direct_finding, field] (Peng 2024, [doi:10.1016/j.srs.2024.100176](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.srs.2024.100176))
- **C1128.** At Galveston Pier 21, estimated subsidence rates changed from 3.53 mm/yr in 1909–1937 to 6.08 mm/yr in 1937–1983 and 3.51 mm/yr thereafter; aquifer compaction contributed as much as 85% of 0.7 m relative sea-level rise since 1909. *Regime: Land subsidence contributions to relative sea level rise at tide gauge Galveston Pier 21, Texas.* [direct_finding, field] (Liu 2020, [doi:10.1038/s41598-020-74696-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-74696-4))
- **C1129.** Across five long-record Oceania tide gauges, the reported mean relative rise was 1.306 mm/yr and mean absolute rise 0.125 mm/yr, with subsidence contributing substantially to local relative trends. *Regime: Relative sea-level rise and land subsidence in Oceania from tide gauge and satellite GPS.* [direct_finding, field] (Boretti 2020, [doi:10.1515/nleng-2020-0007](https://doi.org/10.1515/nleng-2020-0007))
- **C1130.** For China, short or incomplete tide-gauge records and unassessed instrument vertical motion prevented robust separation of absolute sea-level trend from land motion. *Regime: Sea Level Rise and Land Subsidence Contributions to the Signals from the Tide Gauges of China.* [direct_finding, field] (Parker 2016, [doi:10.1515/nleng-2015-0033](https://doi.org/10.1515/nleng-2015-0033))
- **C1382.** San Francisco Bay analysis shows climate-driven sea-level rise and local land subsidence jointly expand inundation exposure beyond estimates that assume a stable land surface. *Regime: Global climate change and local land subsidence exacerbate inundation risk to the San Francisco Bay Area.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Manoochehr Shirzaei 2018, [doi:10.1126/sciadv.aap9234](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aap9234))
- **C1383.** Observed vertical land motion reveals hidden Atlantic-coast vulnerability because subsidence increases relative sea-level rise and future inundation beyond ocean-only projections. *Regime: Hidden vulnerability of US Atlantic coast to sea-level rise due to vertical land motion.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Leonard O. Ohenhen 2023, [doi:10.1038/s41467-023-37853-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-37853-7))
- **C1449.** In many coastal and delta cities, anthropogenic land subsidence exceeds absolute sea-level rise and amplifies flood frequency, depth, duration, and structural damage. *Regime: Sinking coastal cities.* [literature_review_statement, review] (Gilles Erkens 2015, [doi:10.5194/piahs-372-189-2015](https://doi.org/10.5194/piahs-372-189-2015))
- **C1696.** Coastal fluid extraction can make anthropogenic land subsidence the dominant component of relative sea-level rise: documented extremes approach 30 cm per year and more than 13 m cumulatively, far exceeding typical natural sediment compaction and contemporary global sea-level-rise rates. *Regime: Coastal deltas and aquifer systems experiencing substantial groundwater or hydrocarbon extraction..* [literature_review_statement, review] (Ingebritsen 2014, [doi:10.1088/1748-9326/9/9/091002](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/9/9/091002))
