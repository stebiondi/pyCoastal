## Synthesis

**Well established.** Rogue waves are unusually large relative to the surrounding sea state and can result from linear superposition, directional or bathymetric focusing and nonlinear wave-group dynamics; coastal depth variation changes both elevation and velocity extremes.

**Governing physics.** Refraction over shoals and steps concentrates directional energy, shoaling changes steepness and bound harmonics, reflections and currents alter phase coherence, and modulational instability can amplify narrow-banded groups where depth and directional spreading permit it.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include relative depth kh, wave steepness, Benjamin–Feir index, Ursell number, directional spread, bandwidth, current-to-group-speed ratio, bathymetric length relative to wavelength, focusing gain and normalized crest or wave height.

**Major equations.** Descriptions range from linear ray and spectral superposition through nonlinear Schrödinger and higher-order envelope equations to fully nonlinear potential-flow or phase-resolving models. Statistics use exceedance distributions, skewness, kurtosis and crest or wave height normalized by significant height.

**Typical methods.** Studies generate long irregular-wave ensembles, vary spectra, direction and depth transitions, measure surface elevation and three-component velocity, map spatial moments and exceedances, separate bound and free components, test ensemble convergence and compare linear, weakly nonlinear and fully nonlinear predictions.

**Numerical models.** The evidence spans analytical shoaling and wave-group frameworks and simulations/experiments over two-dimensional bathymetry. Direct directional-focusing and fully nonlinear finite-depth model papers remain outside reviewed full text.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence includes irregular waves over a circular shoal and semicircular step with spatial surface and velocity statistics, plus controlled variable-depth experiments summarized in a coastal rogue-wave review.

**Validated ranges.** Support is limited to the tested shoal, step and reviewed controlled depth-transition configurations. Surface and horizontal-velocity kurtosis maxima occurred at different locations, so surface-elevation extremes cannot universally proxy subsurface kinematic demand.

**Recent advances.** Recent advances combine large fully nonlinear ensembles, spatial velocity statistics, variable-depth and current experiments, directional wave-basin control, data-driven rare-event sampling and real-time detection using radar, buoys and platform motion.

**Disagreements.** The relative importance of modulational instability versus linear directional/bathymetric focusing depends strongly on bandwidth, directional spread and depth. Envelope models lose validity for broad spectra, strong breaking or abrupt bathymetry, while linear models omit bound and resonant nonlinearities.

**Limitations.** Rare-event sampling demands long records and many realizations; wave breaking censors the upper tail, laboratory scale and wavemaker recurrence can bias statistics, and sparse gauges miss moving hotspots. Definitions and thresholds for rogue events also vary.

**Open questions.** Priorities include directional finite-depth thresholds, currents and crossing seas, coupled surface/velocity/load extremes, breaking-limited tails, realistic three-dimensional bathymetry, field detection, nonstationary storm ensembles and probabilistic design implications.

**Seminal papers.** Draupner-era observations established the engineering reality of rogue waves; Benjamin–Feir instability and nonlinear Schrödinger theory framed envelope amplification, while directional focusing, higher-order spectra and fully nonlinear simulations broadened mechanisms.
