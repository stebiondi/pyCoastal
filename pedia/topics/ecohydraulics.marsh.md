# Salt marsh dynamics

`ecohydraulics.marsh` | Flow, sediment, and marsh response.

Parent: [Coastal ecosystems and ecohydraulics](ecohydraulics.md)

Papers: 35. Claims: 34. Equations: 0.

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Salt marshes are coupled biogeomorphic systems: vegetation changes drag and routing, which alters sediment delivery and channel form, while elevation and morphology feed back on inundation and plant survival.

**Governing physics.** Controls include tidal prism and asymmetry, surge level, wind waves, creek-edge exchange, sediment concentration and settling, plant frontal area and flexibility, platform elevation, edge geometry, sediment supply, subsidence, and relative sea-level rise.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Useful controls include relative water level and platform elevation, wave-to-current velocity ratio, relative submergence, drag and frontal-area density, settling-to-advection time, sediment supply relative to accommodation, and SLR relative to accretion.

**Major equations.** Frameworks combine shallow-water and wave-current flow, vegetation drag, suspended-sediment conservation, erosion/deposition, Exner-type morphology, tidal storage, edge retreat, plant response, and elevation-dependent accretion.

**Typical methods.** Methods span flux stations, erosion and elevation surveys, channel-network metrics, true-scale surge tests, mown-plot experiments, storm reconstructions, remote topography, and coupled hydro-biogeomorphic modelling.

**Numerical models.** Three-dimensional routing models, estuary storage models, delta sediment models, decadal edge and channel models, restoration biogeomorphology, pond-growth models, and wave-current plant models address complementary scales.

**Experimental datasets.** Evidence includes Venice and Massachusetts networks, confined-estuary scenarios, true-scale surge surfaces, Wax Lake floods, restored marshes, microtidal storm delivery, paired vegetation plots, and cross-marsh topographic signatures.

**Validated ranges.** Reported cases include less than 0.6 cm mean storm-surface lowering, roughly 0.5 cm extra accretion per 60-day flood with stronger tides, doubled marsh flux during a moderate surge, and effects that can reverse sign with sediment supply or scale.

**Recent advances.** Recent studies resolve competing density effects, surge-enabled sediment delivery, tide-wave import switches, topographic storm signatures, creek-complexity resilience, pond intervention windows, and restoration design controls.

**Disagreements.** Vegetation does not universally increase deposition: trapping can be offset by flow buffering, small mown plots showed no local effect, and vegetation simultaneously promotes channelization through drag and channel infilling through enhanced platform accretion.

**Limitations.** Marsh response is scale-, species-, season-, and event-dependent. Sparse storm observations, uncertain belowground and edge processes, simplified plant mechanics, incomplete sediment budgets, and transfer across tidal regimes constrain forecasts.

**Open questions.** Priorities include storm versus fair-weather budgets, creek-edge pathway partitioning, edge failure distributions, vegetation trait and damage dynamics, pond thresholds, subsidence, sediment diversion, restoration geometry, and uncertainty under accelerating SLR.

**Seminal papers.** The branch progresses from marsh-surface flux and vegetation-routing concepts to network efficiency, flexible-plant damping, field-calibrated sediment budgets, and coupled restoration and climate-response models.
