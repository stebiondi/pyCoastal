## Synthesis

**Well established.** Coastal design converts hazards and functional demands into limit states, dimensions, materials, detailing, construction, monitoring and intervention decisions while balancing safety, serviceability, durability, cost, environmental effects and acceptable risk.

**Governing physics.** Design actions arise from waves, currents, tides, surge, overtopping, flooding, erosion and geotechnical change; resistance evolves through structural response, soil interaction, fatigue, corrosion, wear, settlement, ecological succession and cumulative damage.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Important controls include reliability index, utilization and safety factors, return-period and exceedance probability, damage and serviceability indices, relative freeboard, wave steepness and surf similarity, armor stability numbers, corrosion or fatigue life ratios, redundancy, recovery time and benefit-cost ratios.

**Major equations.** Core formulations include action–resistance limit states, partial-factor or reliability equations, joint-probability load combinations, hydraulic and structural response relations, deterioration and condition-transition models, lifecycle cost or utility, benefit-cost and risk integrals, and adaptation decision rules.

**Typical methods.** Practice combines standards and consequence classes, deterministic and probabilistic design, physical and numerical modelling, field monitoring, condition assessment, failure-mode analysis, lifecycle optimization, multi-criteria appraisal, stakeholder and ecological assessment, and adaptive pathways.

**Numerical models.** Tools span spectral and phase-resolving hydrodynamics, overtopping and inundation models, geotechnical and structural finite elements, reliability and Bayesian networks, deterioration and asset-management models, lifecycle optimization, digital asset records and climate-adaptation scenarios.

**Experimental datasets.** Evidence comes from material and component tests, hydraulic model tests, prototype storm and damage records, long-term defence and structure monitoring, inspection and maintenance histories, failure databases, environmental performance, and infrastructure risk inventories.

**Validated ranges.** The screened evidence covers reinforced-concrete coastal structures, river and coastal bioengineering, Dutch flood defences, coastal railway exposure and offshore multi-system infrastructure; each transfers only within its asset, hazard, jurisdiction, material and governance regime.

**Recent advances.** Recent practice integrates probabilistic flood-defence management, climate-risk screening of infrastructure corridors, durable low-carbon materials, ecological engineering, multi-system offshore design, monitoring-informed asset management and flexible adaptation pathways.

**Disagreements.** Deterministic factors and probabilistic reliability encode uncertainty differently; least first cost can conflict with lifecycle reliability and environmental benefit; hard protection can reduce one failure mode while shifting erosion or ecological impacts; robust near-term actions may differ from expected-value optima.

**Limitations.** Joint hazard tails, deterioration, hidden defects, failure dependence, construction quality, maintenance behavior, ecosystem trajectories, indirect losses and future climate remain weakly observed; standards may lag nonstationarity and institutional responsibilities can fragment system performance.

**Open questions.** Priorities include nonstationary joint hazards, cascading and network failure, observation-updated reliability, hybrid and nature-based limit states, embodied carbon and biodiversity, constructability, autonomous inspection, adaptive design triggers, equity and transparent deep-uncertainty decisions.

**Seminal papers.** Classical coastal design established return-period loading, hydraulic similitude, armor and overtopping formulae, limit-state checks and safety factors; reliability and lifecycle methods later formalized uncertainty, consequences, deterioration and intervention timing.
