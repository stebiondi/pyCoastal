# Beaches and shoreline evolution

`beaches` | Beach state and shoreline change.

Subtopics: [Shoreline change](beaches.change.md), [Dunes and overwash](beaches.dunes.md), [Beach nourishment](beaches.nourishment.md), [Storm-driven beach response](beaches.storms.md)

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Beaches are open sediment systems whose shoreline and profile evolve through coupled cross-shore and longshore transport, changing water levels, sediment sources and sinks, antecedent morphology and human interventions.

**Governing physics.** Wave and current stresses move sediment; water level controls where energy is delivered; gradients in longshore transport change planform; cross-shore exchange reshapes profiles; rivers, cliffs, shelf stores, dunes and engineering alter the budget; morphology feeds back on forcing.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include wave steepness and direction, relative water level, surf similarity, fall-velocity parameter, closure depth, profile slope, sediment size, transport-gradient and recovery time scales, compartment leakage and sediment-input/transport-capacity ratios.

**Major equations.** Core formulations are Exner sediment continuity, one-line shoreline continuity, equilibrium-profile and disequilibrium response, cross-/longshore transport laws, sediment-budget mass balance, state-space/data assimilation and coupled shelf–shoreline models.

**Typical methods.** Methods combine repeated topobathymetry, satellite shorelines, lidar/UAS, sediment tracing and budgets, wave/water-level records, trend segmentation, equilibrium and process models, multi-model frameworks, data assimilation and scenario analysis.

**Numerical models.** Represented tools include shoreline trend clustering, satellite-assimilative dynamic shoreline models, sediment-budget calculators, coupled cross-/longshore and shelf–shoreline models, equilibrium response, mesoscale multi-model frameworks and climate-driver attribution.

**Experimental datasets.** Evidence includes Australia-wide Landsat coastlines, global satellite shorelines, the Elwha dam-removal coast, West African and Ebro sediment systems, Shoalhaven compartments and modeled shelf-ridge/shoreline coupling.

**Validated ranges.** The corpus spans sandy open coasts and barriers, adjacent managed compartments, river- and port-modified littoral cells, low-gradient deltas, seasonal through multi-decadal records, national mapping and global climate variability.

**Recent advances.** Recent work uses decades of satellite shorelines, data assimilation, cluster-based reaches, global climate attribution, intervention natural experiments, coupled shelf–shoreline models and hierarchical management compartments.

**Disagreements.** A shoreline line alone may indicate retreat while subaerial volume or profile storage behaves differently; equilibrium models are efficient but miss pathways and memory; local trends may reflect basin climate rather than secular forcing; fixed cells may leak sediment.

**Limitations.** Shoreline proxies and tidal correction introduce error; bathymetric records are sparse; sediment budgets rarely close; recovery may exceed monitoring duration; intervention histories are incomplete; model parameters are nonstationary and scale-dependent.

**Open questions.** Priorities include profile–planform coupling, shelf exchange, event recovery and memory, mixed sediment, compound water levels, human intervention attribution, dynamic compartment boundaries and probabilistic multi-decadal forecasting.

**Seminal papers.** Profile and one-line shoreline models established reduced beach evolution, while sediment budgets and littoral-cell concepts established source–pathway–sink management.
