# Nature-based coastal protection

`nature_based` | Risk reduction using natural or hybrid systems.

Subtopics: [Dune-based protection](nature_based.dunes.md), [Hybrid defenses](nature_based.hybrid.md), [Reefs and living shorelines](nature_based.reefs.md), [Vegetation and wave attenuation](nature_based.vegetation.md)

Papers: 20. Claims: 2. Equations: 0.

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Reefs, wetlands, dunes and submerged vegetation can reduce waves, currents, erosion and flood consequences while providing habitat, carbon and other services. Performance is conditional on geometry, elevation, health, forcing and sediment context and may be enhanced by hybrid structural elements.

**Governing physics.** Canopy and reef drag dissipate wave energy; roughness, breaking, reflection and freeboard control transmission. Vegetation and shellfish alter sediment deposition and soil strength, while growth, recruitment, mortality and accommodation determine whether protective geometry persists under sea-level rise.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include relative freeboard, submergence, width-to-wavelength ratio, frontal-area density, drag and transmission coefficients, wave steepness, habitat inundation fraction, growth-to-sea-level-rise ratio, sediment accretion balance and benefit-cost or service-value ratios.

**Major equations.** Design uses wave-energy and momentum balances with vegetation or reef drag, transmission and overtopping relations, sediment budgets and ecological growth or survival constraints. Risk and valuation frameworks combine avoided damage, lifecycle cost, service flows and co-benefits under uncertain performance.

**Typical methods.** Studies combine flumes and phase-resolving models with before–after/control field designs, wave and morphology monitoring, habitat and recruitment surveys, remote sensing, ecosystem-service valuation and participatory planning. Goal-based monitoring should separately test physical, ecological and social objectives.

**Numerical models.** Models range from drag and transmission formulations to FUNWAVE-TVD and idealized multi-habitat profiles, shoreline and sediment models, ecosystem-growth projections and spatial ecosystem-service planning. Coupled models must preserve both hydrodynamic and viability constraints.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence includes replicated oyster breakwaters, four-season Bangladesh monitoring, Atlantic/Gulf oyster comparisons, seven years at Gandy's Beach, Belize planning scenarios, 59 European cases, Port Phillip kelp sites and restored-coral canopy flume tests.

**Validated ranges.** Examples are site-specific: oyster reefs reduced retreat over 40% at one site, Bangladesh erosion 54%, low-inundation reefs attenuated 68% versus about 5% for oyster-suitable reefs, and restored coral canopies dissipated over 50% under typical tested reef-flat conditions.

**Recent advances.** Recent work uses long-term multidisciplinary monitoring, force-resolved coral-canopy experiments, phase-resolving living-shoreline models, multi-habitat portfolios, adaptive-growth constraints and participatory service optimization rather than protection-only design.

**Disagreements.** Ecological success does not guarantee engineering success: seven-year oyster reefs met habitat but not protection targets, and the freeboard most effective for attenuation can be unsuitable for oysters. Kelp also failed to outperform barren controls at most tested sites, countering assumptions that any vegetation necessarily protects.

**Limitations.** Short monitoring, survivorship and maturity uncertainty, scale effects, changing freeboard, storm scarcity, incomplete sediment budgets, inconsistent terminology and metrics, omitted failure modes and weak disclosure of quantitative effectiveness limit transfer and financing.

**Open questions.** Priorities include lifecycle failure and recovery, adaptive growth under rapid sea-level rise, optimal hybrid geometry, extreme-event performance, sediment feedback, multi-habitat sequencing, standardized monitoring, equitable governance and bankable valuation of co-benefits.

**Seminal papers.** Early wetland, reef and vegetation studies established attenuation and erosion services; ecosystem-service frameworks connected those functions to risk and value. Living-shoreline and hybrid concepts then integrated habitat restoration with engineered stabilization.

## Claims

- **C1194.** Nature-based coastal protection should be treated as conditional risk-reduction infrastructure whose physical performance, recovery capacity, co-benefits and compatibility with hybrid defenses are evaluated together. *Regime: Coastal ecosystems considered for wave, surge, erosion, and profile-maintenance services..* [literature_review_statement, review] (Mark Spalding 2014, [doi:10.1111/conl.12074](https://doi.org/10.1111/conl.12074))
- **C1406.** A synthesis compares effectiveness, costs and coastal-protection benefits of natural and nature-based defenses and identifies evidence and valuation needs for risk-reduction decisions. *Regime: The Effectiveness, Costs and Coastal Protection Benefits of Natural and Nature-Based Defences.* [literature_review_statement, review] (Siddharth Narayan 2016, [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0154735](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0154735))
