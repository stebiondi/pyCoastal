# Sea-level projections

`climate.projections` | Global-to-local sea-level scenarios.

Parent: [Sea-level rise and climate adaptation](climate.md)

Papers: 11. Claims: 5. Equations: 0.

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Coastal engineering must treat mean sea level as nonstationary over asset lifetimes. Global trajectories require localization for land motion and regional ocean effects, then combination with tides, surge, waves, morphology, exposure, and vulnerability before they become design criteria.

**Governing physics.** Thermal expansion and land-ice loss drive global mean rise, while ocean circulation, gravitational fingerprints, tides, vertical land motion, waves, surge, sediment response, and groundwater processes shape local relative level and impact.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Useful controls include exceedance probability, relative sea-level change normalized by freeboard or crest height, crest-rise amplification, relative wave height and runup, discount and maintenance fractions, and ensemble weights or scenario probabilities.

**Major equations.** Engineering translations include products of time-varying annual-maximum distributions, scenario crest-rise ratios, runup and barrier-stability relations, depth-damage functions, Monte Carlo expectation, and Bellman recursion for sequential adaptation.

**Typical methods.** Methods span tide gauges and satellite altimetry, process-based or scenario projections, localization with land motion, nonstationary extreme-value analysis, profile and runup models, GIS exposure inventories, Monte Carlo damage simulation, cost-benefit optimization, and adaptive-pathway planning.

**Numerical models.** Models include multi-distribution nonstationary extremes, the Shingle Beach Run-up Tool, Barrier Inertia Model, stochastic depth-damage simulation, and dynamic adaptation optimization; they are consequence translators rather than interchangeable sea-level projection generators.

**Experimental datasets.** The screened engineering evidence uses Adelaide annual maxima, four southeast England beach profiles and a 2014 validation storm, a Bay County building and flood inventory, and more than three decades of multi-mission altimetry; these datasets support different links in the projection-to-decision chain.

**Validated ranges.** Reported applications include four southeast England beaches under 1-5 m scenarios with crest rise up to 1.26 times SLR, a 200-year joint wave-water-level standard, four SLR trajectories in Bay County, and a location-shift extreme model demonstrated for Adelaide; none constitutes a universal design factor.

**Recent advances.** Recent engineering work couples multiple SLR trajectories to stochastic damages and revisable actions, while coastal design studies resolve how local wave transformation and morphology can amplify physical requirements beyond the imposed mean rise.

**Disagreements.** Differences among design outcomes often reflect scenario choice, localization, impact model, asset lifetime, discounting, and adaptation objective rather than disagreement about whether sea level is rising. A deterministic high scenario can stress-test feasibility but is not itself a probabilistic forecast.

**Limitations.** The branch lacks a full primary projection ensemble and ice-sheet synthesis. Local vertical land motion, changing surge and wave climatology, datum consistency, compound flooding, sediment supply, model structural error, socioeconomic change, and deep uncertainty remain incompletely propagated into most engineering decisions.

**Open questions.** Priorities include traceable global-to-local probabilistic workflows, nonstationary extremes beyond simple location shifts, compound-hazard projections, observational signposts for adaptive pathways, equitable financing, sediment-aware defense adaptation, and validation of decisions as sea level and exposure evolve.

**Seminal papers.** Within this screened slice, the 1990 paper is seminal for replacing stationary return-period logic with time-varying annual distributions, providing an early direct bridge from projected mean rise to future design extremes.

## Claims

- **C1139.** The projected global-average 100-year extreme sea level rises 34–76 cm by 2100 under a moderate-mitigation scenario and 58–172 cm under business as usual, making present 100-year levels annual across much of the tropics from about 2050. *Regime: Global probabilistic projections of extreme sea levels show intensification of coastal flood hazard.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Michalis Vousdoukas 2018, [doi:10.1038/s41467-018-04692-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-04692-w))
- **C1145.** A comprehensive review links observed and projected sea-level rise to its climatic causes, global and Japanese impacts, and societal response options while identifying unresolved projection and adaptation questions. *Regime: Sea-level rise caused by climate change and its implications for society.* [direct_finding, mixed] (Nobuo Mimura 2013, [doi:10.2183/pjab.89.281](https://doi.org/10.2183/pjab.89.281))
- **C1361.** A sea-level review defines and links local, global, mean, variable and changing sea-level concepts with physical and mathematical terminology intended to prevent ambiguity across research and policy. *Regime: Concepts and Terminology for Sea Level: Mean, Variability and Change, Both Local and Global.* [literature_review_statement, review] (Jonathan M. Gregory 2019, [doi:10.1007/s10712-019-09525-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10712-019-09525-z))
- **C1381.** Global coastal sea-level projections under warming above 2°C show regionally varying relative rise that depends on ocean, ice, gravity and land-motion contributions. *Regime: Coastal sea level rise with warming above 2 °C.* [direct_finding, numerical] (Svetlana Jevrejeva 2016, [doi:10.1073/pnas.1605312113](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605312113))
- **C1736.** Emulated multi-model projections estimate that limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius nearly halves median 2100 land-ice sea-level contribution relative to current pledges, while Antarctic uncertainty leaves coastal adaptation facing roughly a factor-of-three range between central low-warming and risk-averse current-policy cases. *Regime: Global land-ice contribution to mean sea level from 2016-2100 under five Shared Socioeconomic Pathways and a current nationally determined contributions scenario..* [direct_finding, numerical] (Tamsin Edwards 2021, [doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03302-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03302-y))
