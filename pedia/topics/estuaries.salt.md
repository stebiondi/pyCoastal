# Salinity intrusion and mixing

`estuaries.salt` | Salt transport and stratification.

Parent: [Estuaries and tidal inlets](estuaries.md)

Papers: 32. Claims: 31. Equations: 0.

## Synthesis

**Well established.** Estuarine salinity reflects a dynamic balance between landward ocean salt transport and seaward river flushing, mediated by gravitational exchange, tidal oscillatory transport, stratification-dependent mixing, dispersion and geometry.

**Governing physics.** Longitudinal density gradients drive landward bottom flow and seaward surface flow; tides alternately strain and mix salinity; river discharge advects salt seaward; wind, waves, ocean salinity, sea level, branching and constrictions alter circulation and mixing.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include estuarine Froude and Richardson numbers, tidal excursion/estuary length, freshwater Froude, mixing/stratification parameters, Simpson number, discharge/tidal prism, convergence length, drag, X2 position, sea-level/depth ratio and wind stress.

**Major equations.** The salt budget uses advection–diffusion conservation with resolved or effective dispersion. Exchange decompositions separate mean, tidal oscillatory and shear transports; intrusion models link length to discharge, tidal velocity, convergence and dispersion; tracer-variance decay diagnoses irreversible mixing.

**Typical methods.** Methods include longitudinal and cross-channel CTD/ADCP surveys, fixed salinity and microstructure time series, tracer variance, box and one-dimensional models, three-dimensional hydrostatic models, empirical intrusion equations, scenario ensembles and global forcing attribution.

**Numerical models.** Models include UnTRIM, FVCOM, coupled ocean–wave systems, primitive-equation estuary models, time-dependent box models, integrated coastal–inland delta models, effective-dispersion salt-wedge models and global scenario frameworks.

**Experimental datasets.** Evidence spans Willapa, Puget/Salish, Pearl, Mekong, Sumjin, San Francisco, Hudson, Yarra, Strymon and Rhine–Meuse systems, plus idealized barrier/constriction cases and a global estuary ensemble.

**Validated ranges.** The corpus covers well-mixed, partially mixed, periodically stratified and salt-wedge regimes; microtidal to macrotidal systems; single and branching channels; normal flow, drought and near-zero discharge; storm barriers, constrictions and future sea level.

**Recent advances.** Recent work uses tracer-variance mixing, lateral transport theory, sub-estuary networks, drought observations, coupled wind–wave effects, integrated delta models and global attribution of compounding future salt intrusion.

**Disagreements.** One-dimensional effective dispersion can predict intrusion length but obscures lateral and temporal mechanisms; equilibrium formulas miss transient memory; greater depth from sea-level rise can either enhance intrusion or alter tides/mixing enough to offset it, depending on geometry.

**Limitations.** Sparse cross-channel observations limit lateral-flux closure; mixing is intermittent; bathymetry and roughness evolve; discharge and ocean salinity boundaries are uncertain; empirical coefficients are site-dependent; global projections simplify local structures and operations.

**Open questions.** Needs include transient memory and hysteresis, branching deltas, lateral transport, barrier operations, morphology feedback, compound drought–surge–sea-level forcing, ocean salinity change, data assimilation and probabilistic freshwater thresholds.

**Seminal papers.** Classical adjustment, salt-flux and effective-dispersion studies established river–tide control and gravitational exchange; long field records then revealed unsteadiness, lateral circulation and intermittent stratification.
