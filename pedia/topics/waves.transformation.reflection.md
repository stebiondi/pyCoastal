## Synthesis

**Well established.** Coastal wave reflection redirects energy from beaches, structures and depth transitions; reflected and incident components interfere to form standing or partial-standing patterns, alter directional spectra and redistribute harbor agitation even when bulk significant height is similar.

**Governing physics.** Reflection depends on impedance change, slope, roughness, porosity, structure geometry, water depth, frequency and incidence angle. Diffraction spreads reflected energy around boundaries, while nonlinear interactions and wave grouping create low-frequency components that need not be harbor resonance.

**Dimensionless parameters.** Controls include reflection coefficient, kh, surf-similarity, relative roughness and permeability, incidence angle, structure slope and freeboard, frequency-band energy fraction, standing-wave ratio and geometry or basin length relative to wavelength.

**Major equations.** Reflection coefficients use reflected-to-incident amplitude or energy; gauge-array methods solve incident/reflected spectral components, and momentum-flux approaches enforce force or radiation-stress balances. Mild-slope, potential-flow and phase-resolving equations represent coupled reflection and diffraction.

**Typical methods.** Studies use multi-gauge arrays or directional spectra to separate incident and reflected energy, test regular and irregular seas, map harbor spatial spectra, compare frequency bands and directions, validate against structures or bathymetry and check whether low-frequency energy is bound, reflected or resonant.

**Numerical models.** The reviewed branch includes spectral-type reconstruction and experimental transfer coefficients. Direct third-order and momentum-flux reflection analyses remain in the lawful-full-text queue; common predictive frameworks include mild-slope, boundary-integral and phase-resolving models.

**Experimental datasets.** Reviewed evidence includes a 40-year multimodal outer-port/harbor spectral reconstruction for Africa basin and physical harbor-model experiments comparing regular and irregular diffraction-reflection transfer behavior.

**Validated ranges.** Evidence is limited to the studied harbor configurations and dominant incident-energy bands. In irregular tests, coefficients varied less than regular-wave values within the main band; Africa-basin energetic patterns were linked to Reina Sofia breakwater reflection.

**Recent advances.** Recent advances use dense radar/video and pressure arrays, Bayesian directional separation, high-order and fully nonlinear solvers, porous and flexible boundaries, data-assimilative harbor digital twins and spectral-type clustering that retains reflected multimodality.

**Disagreements.** Regular-wave tests reveal sharp frequency structure but may exaggerate sensitivity relative to broadband seas. Bulk height and period can hide directional reflection and multimodality, while large transfer estimates outside energetic bands may be statistically unstable yet energetically minor.

**Limitations.** Incident/reflected separation is ill-conditioned with poor gauge spacing, nonlinear waves, multiple directions or evanescent modes. Sparse spatial measurements, uncertain boundary spectra, diffraction, harbor resonance and changing water level complicate attribution.

**Open questions.** Priorities include robust multidirectional separation, nonlinear and third-order reflection, currents, porous and overtopped structures, field-scale phase observations, reflection under changing water levels and morphology and uncertainty-aware links to loads and navigation.

**Seminal papers.** Standing-wave gauge methods established laboratory reflection measurement; linear potential-flow and mild-slope theory treated structures and bathymetry, while directional spectral arrays and irregular-wave transfer functions extended analysis to realistic seas and harbors.
